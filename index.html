The quality audit cycle explained: from taking part to leading the audit

A quality audit asks a simple question: does the work actually meet the standard, procedure or policy it is supposed to meet? Answering that question well is a skill. It takes planning, a careful eye for evidence and the confidence to report findings that people may not want to hear. This guide walks through the audit cycle and explains how the BSB50920 Diploma of Quality Auditing is structured around it.

Stage one: planning the audit

Every audit starts with scope. The auditor confirms what is being audited, against which criteria, and why. Criteria might be an internal procedure, a customer contract, a management system standard or a regulatory requirement. The auditor then works out what evidence will be needed, who needs to be interviewed and which records should be sampled. A clear plan protects both the auditor and the people being audited, because everyone knows in advance what will be examined.

Stage two: gathering evidence

On the day, the auditor collects objective evidence. That can mean reviewing documents and records, observing work as it happens and interviewing the people who do it. Good auditors ask open questions, listen carefully and record exactly what they see. The aim is not to catch people out. It is to build an accurate picture of how the process works in practice compared with how it is meant to work.

Stage three: evaluating findings

Once the evidence is in, the auditor compares it against the criteria. Some findings are clear conformities. Others are gaps, where the process does not meet the requirement, or observations, where something works now but carries a risk. Each finding needs to be traceable to evidence, so that anyone reading the report can see why the conclusion was reached.

Stage four: reporting and follow-up

The audit report is the product that people act on. It should state the scope, summarise the findings, and set out any nonconformities in plain language. Follow-up matters just as much: an audit that identifies a problem but never checks whether it was fixed adds little value. Many organisations track corrective actions until they are verified as closed.

Leading an audit team

On larger audits the work is shared. A lead auditor coordinates the team, allocates areas, keeps the audit to its plan and brings the findings together into a single, consistent report. That role calls for communication and judgement as well as technical knowledge.

How the BSB50920 Diploma of Quality Auditing fits

The BSB50920 Diploma of Quality Auditing is a nationally recognised AQF Level 5 qualification. training.gov.au describes it as suited to people with a sound theoretical knowledge of quality auditing who plan, carry out and evaluate their own work or the work of an audit team. Its core units follow the cycle above, from taking part in audits to initiating, leading and reporting on them. Electives come from Audit and Compliance and from Transferable Skills, so the course can be shaped around your workplace.

Assessment is by practical workplace tasks, not exams, and you do not need a lower qualification first. To enrol you need either a formal qualification or a short assessment of language, literacy, numeracy and digital skills. For people who want a smaller step, a lead auditor skill set leads to a Statement of Attainment for the units completed.
